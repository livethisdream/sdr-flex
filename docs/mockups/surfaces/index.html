How many surfaces?

The layout study got chrome down to about 90 px and then stopped paying. What is left to win is not height — it is the number of separate things on screen and the number of different menus behind them. This one is clickable, because a popunder and a hover are behaviors and a screenshot cannot say whether they feel worse than what they replace.

Try: click a crumb for the map · hover the channel crumb for the tuner's stats · i pins that card open · drag a box on the spectrum for operations · click the spectrum without dragging for view options · right-click for the node's own menu · space pauses.

The strip splits three ways; it is not replaced by a hover. An earlier pass here collapsed all of it onto the hover card, which was more than the idea asked for. The three kinds of parameter want three different homes: the tuner's numbers are mostly evidence — you set center and width by dragging the box — so they can be summoned from the crumb. View options go in the click menu, where the picture they change is already under the cursor. A block's working parameters — a slicer threshold, a symbol period — are the ones you scrub while watching the result, and those belong on the object: a threshold is a line on the waveform, a symbol period is a spacing on the time axis. That last one is described here, not built.

What it costs and what it buys

Struck-through rows are surfaces that stop existing. Nothing here is free: the two rows that disappear were also the inventory of what you had built, and the strip was the only place the auto/manual edge was visible without asking.

These are prior reasoning to weigh, not rules to comply with — but they were written after someone hit the problem, so the cost is worth naming before spending it. Pointer travel, which is the metric that matters

The first version of this page led with a click count, and a click count is misleading: two clicks thirty pixels apart are not two clicks at opposite ends of the window. 08-ui-principles says this itself — “counting only clicks would have scored the old pinned-sidebar layout as equal to the contextual one. It was not equal — it charged a ~600 px round trip for every single operation.”

So here is the same comparison done properly. Both layouts are built at the same size from the app's own markup, the surfaces each task actually opens are rendered rather than imagined, and the path is summed in real pixels. The cursor starts where it lives: on the signal, low in the waterfall. Budget is < 120 px per operation after a selection drag.

The colormap settles the argument about clicks. The proposal takes one more click and 152 px less travel, and it is plainly the better one. A click count scored that as a regression.

Both mouse losses have one cause, and it is not the popunder. Reading the tuner's numbers costs 229 px more and switching channel 260 px more — because in each case the thing moved from the foot of the screen to the head, and the cursor lives low in the waterfall. That is the same result the layout study got when it tried putting the strip along the top row. Twice now, the top of the screen has measured as the expensive place to put something you reach for.

Is that an artifact of where the cursor starts?

Two of those five results depend on a number I chose — the cursor starting low in the waterfall. So it is swept instead: ?sy=0.20 starts on the spectrum trace, ?sy=0.85 deep in the waterfall. Travel in pixels, today → proposed:

One column does not move. The colormap costs 234 px from anywhere, because the menu arrives at the cursor. Every other column slides with the cursor, because every other target is pinned to a bar. That is the whole finding, and it is stronger than any single row: a surface that comes to the cursor has no travel cost to argue about; a surface anchored to chrome has one that grows the further you are from it.

It also corrects the previous conclusion. “The top of the screen is expensive” was overstated — reading the width costs 88 px more with the cursor on the trace, not 229. But switching channel loses by a flat ~260 px at every start point, and that one is real: the map popunder is anchored to the bar, so it inherits exactly the problem the menu was designed to avoid.

So the trigger is wrong, not the surface. Hovering a crumb drags the cursor to the top of the window to read something about the signal underneath it. The hotkey does not: the card and the map arrive at the cursor, which is why the key column wins every row and puts four of five tasks inside the 120 px budget. The crumb hover should stay as the discoverable path — it is how you find out the card exists — but it is the slow one, and the design should not be judged on it.
